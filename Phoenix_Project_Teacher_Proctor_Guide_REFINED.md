# The Phoenix Project Teacher / Proctor Guide

## 4-Week Brown-Bag Study Series

**Book:** *The Phoenix Project: A Novel About IT, DevOps, and Helping Your Business Win* by Gene Kim, Kevin Behr, and George Spafford  
**Audience:** Project Managers, Product Owners, Developers, Business Analysts, Operations, Security, QA, Support, and Leaders  
**Format:** Four 60-minute weekly brown-bag sessions  
**Primary Outcome:** Participants connect the story to their own work system and identify one practical 30-day improvement.

---

## Facilitator Purpose

The facilitator is not expected to lecture through the book chapter by chapter. The goal is to help participants recognize organizational patterns and apply the lessons to their role.

The core shift you are guiding is:

> From: “That happened in the book.”  
> To: “That happens in our organization, and here is what we can improve.”

Use the book as a mirror for common enterprise problems:

- Invisible work
- Unplanned work
- Conflicting priorities
- Bottlenecks and hero culture
- Poor handoffs
- Late security, quality, and operational input
- Weak feedback loops
- Lack of shared business context
- Firefighting instead of continuous improvement

---

## Course Overview

### Course Goal

Participants will understand the principles of DevOps, identify dysfunctions in traditional IT environments, and learn how Lean manufacturing principles can apply to knowledge work.

## Facilitation Ground Rules

Open Week 1 by setting expectations.

1. **Discuss systems, not personalities.** Focus on conditions that create behavior.
2. **Use real examples carefully.** Protect sensitive names, incidents, and systems.
3. **Challenge respectfully.** The goal is learning, not winning an argument.
4. **Avoid complaint-only discussion.** Convert complaints into problem statements.
5. **Connect every concept to action.** Ask, “What could we try differently?”
6. **Think cross-functionally.** The book is not only about IT Operations.

Useful facilitator language:

> “Let’s turn that into a system problem. What condition allows that to keep happening?”

> “Who is impacted downstream when that handoff is unclear?”

> “What would make this work visible earlier?”

## Major Concepts to Reinforce Throughout

### The Four Types of Work

The book identifies four major types of IT work:

1. **Business Projects** — work tied directly to business initiatives, such as Project Phoenix.
2. **Internal IT Projects** — infrastructure, upgrades, migrations, platform work, and system modernization.
3. **Changes** — updates or modifications to existing systems, often generated by the first two categories.
4. **Unplanned Work** — incidents, break-fixes, outages, emergency requests, escalations, and rework.

Teaching point:

> Unplanned work is often described as “anti-work” because it prevents the completion of planned, value-adding work.

### The Three Ways

| Way | Plain-Language Meaning | Central Question |
|---|---|---|
| First Way | Improve flow from left to right | How does work move from idea to value? |
| Second Way | Create fast feedback from right to left | How quickly do we learn something is wrong? |
| Third Way | Build continual learning and experimentation | How do we improve the system over time? |

### Theory of Constraints

Any system is limited by its slowest constraint. Improving non-constraints may create local improvement without improving the whole system.

The five focusing steps:

1. Identify the constraint.
2. Exploit the constraint by ensuring it only works on what only it can do.
3. Subordinate other work to the constraint.
4. Elevate the constraint by adding capacity or capability.
5. Repeat the process once the constraint moves.

### Brent as the Bottleneck

Brent is the indispensable engineer who becomes the primary bottleneck. He can fix almost anything, so everyone bypasses normal systems and goes directly to him. This makes him a hero and a single point of failure.

The teaching point is not “Brent is bad.” The teaching point is:

> A system that depends on one hero is fragile by design.

### Security Shifting Left

John begins as a traditional compliance gatekeeper. Over time, he learns that security must be integrated into daily delivery workflows rather than appearing only as a late-stage blocker.

Security becomes more effective when it is:

- Introduced during design
- Standardized through reusable control patterns
- Automated where possible
- Connected to real operational risk
- Treated as part of quality and resilience

### Chaos Monkey and Resilience

The Chaos Monkey concept introduces controlled failure as a way to build resilience. The point is not recklessness. The point is practicing recovery before real failure occurs.

Teaching point:

> Resilient systems are not systems that never fail. They are systems that can recover, learn, and improve.

# Week 1 Facilitator Guide

## Week 1: Identifying the Mess and the Four Types of Work

**Reading Assignment:** Chapters 1–10  
**Session Focus:** Baseline dysfunction, unplanned work, hidden work, blame, and making invisible work visible.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of Week 1, participants should be able to:

- Recognize symptoms of a siloed organization.
- Explain the Four Types of Work.
- Describe why unplanned work damages progress.
- Identify examples of invisible work in their own environment.
- Discuss how blame prevents learning.

---

## Opening Prompt

Ask:

> “What part of the opening chapters felt most familiar to your own work environment?”

Let several participants respond before moving into teaching points.

---

## Key Plot Points to Surface

- Bill Palmer is promoted into a role he did not seek.
- Parts Unlimited is struggling with outages, missed commitments, and political pressure.
- Project Phoenix is already in crisis.
- Development, Operations, Security, and the business operate in silos.
- Work is poorly understood, poorly prioritized, and often invisible.
- The organization often blames individuals rather than examining system conditions.

---

## Key Teaching Points

### 1. The Organization Is Busy but Not Effective

Participants may notice that everyone is working hard. This is important. The early dysfunction is not caused by laziness. It is caused by a broken system of work.

Ask:

- What are people working on?
- Who decides what matters most?
- What work is interrupting planned work?
- What work is not visible to leadership?

### 2. Unplanned Work Consumes Capacity

Unplanned work includes outages, break-fixes, escalations, urgent access needs, rework, and emergency changes.

Use this line:

> “Unplanned work does not merely add work. It steals capacity from planned work.”

### 3. Blame Hides Root Causes

If discussion becomes team-blaming, redirect toward system causes.

Instead of:

> “Operations always blocks delivery.”

Ask:

> “What information does Operations receive too late to support delivery safely?”

Instead of:

> “Development throws bad code over the wall.”

Ask:

> “What feedback is missing before code reaches Operations?”

---

## Discussion Questions

1. What are the initial symptoms of dysfunction between Development, IT Operations, Security, and the business?
2. How does Bill initially react to his promotion, and why does he feel that way?
3. What are the Four Types of Work that Erik introduces to Bill?
4. What happens during the Project Phoenix rollout?
5. What are the immediate consequences of bypassing standard IT protocols?
6. What examples of invisible work appear in these chapters?
7. How does finger-pointing prevent the organization from learning?

---

Facilitator move:

Prompt:

> “How does unplanned work act as anti-work?”

Guide them toward:

- Context switching

- Stress
- Quality issues
- More incidents
- Lost trust

# Week 2 Facilitator Guide

## Week 2: The First Way and Managing the Bottleneck

**Reading Assignment:** Chapters 11–20  
**Session Focus:** Theory of Constraints, limiting Work in Progress, Kanban, making work visible, and establishing flow.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of Week 2, participants should be able to:

- Explain the First Way.
- Describe the Theory of Constraints.
- Identify a bottleneck or constraint in a work process.
- Explain why too much Work in Progress slows delivery.
- Describe how Kanban helps make invisible work visible.

---

## Opening Prompt

Ask:

> “What changed once Bill’s team started making work visible?”

---

## Key Plot Points to Surface

- Bill begins applying manufacturing plant principles to IT.
- Brent is identified as the primary bottleneck.
- The team starts visualizing work using Kanban.
- Work in Progress becomes visible.
- The organization begins protecting the constraint.
- The team starts moving from reactive firefighting toward managed flow.

---

## Key Teaching Points

### 1. IT Work Has Inventory

In manufacturing, inventory may be parts on a factory floor. In IT, inventory is often invisible:

- Tickets
- Emails
- Requirements
- Unfinished code
- Waiting approvals
- Unreviewed access requests
- Unresolved incidents
- Unreleased features

Teaching point:

> Just because knowledge work is invisible does not mean it has no inventory.

### 2. Kanban Makes Work Visible

Kanban helps teams see:

- What is being worked
- What is waiting
- Where work is blocked
- Where WIP is too high
- Where handoffs are failing

### 3. Brent Is the Constraint

Brent becomes the constraint because too much work depends on his knowledge and action.

Management does not solve this by firing Brent. They solve it by:

- Identifying him as the constraint
- Protecting him from unnecessary interruption
- Routing work through proper intake
- Ensuring he works only on tasks only he can do
- Transferring knowledge to others
- Creating standards and documentation

### 4. Full Utilization Creates Queues

A fully utilized person or team has no slack for variation, learning, improvement, or urgent issues. Full utilization often increases wait time and reduces quality.

# Week 3 Facilitator Guide

## Week 3: The Second Way and Shifting Left

**Reading Assignment:** Chapters 21–29

## Learning Objectives

- Discuss how Dev and Ops begin to collaborate.
- Explain how security can shift left and become an enabler.

---

Ask:

> “Where in our work do we usually discover problems too late?”

---

## Key Plot Points to Surface

- Development and Operations begin aligning goals.
- Batch sizes are reduced.
- Feedback loops become faster.
- Security begins moving from gatekeeper to integrated partner.
- The team begins improving deployment safety and quality.

---

## Key Teaching Points

### 1. The Second Way Is Feedback

The Second Way focuses on fast feedback from right to left.

In plain language:

> The faster we learn something is wrong, the cheaper and easier it is to fix.

Examples of feedback:

- Automated tests
- Monitoring alerts
- Security scans
- Peer review
- Incident reviews
- Stakeholder demos
- Deployment results
- Support ticket trends

### 2. Smaller Batches Reduce Risk

Large releases hide problems until late. Smaller batches make it easier to test, deploy, troubleshoot, and recover.

Ask:

- What is our version of a large batch?
- Large project phase?
- Big-bang release?
- Quarterly access review?
- Massive change window?
- Huge requirements package?

### 3. Dev and Ops Collaboration Requires Shared Goals

At the start, Development is incentivized to push features and Operations is incentivized to preserve stability. This creates conflict.

Collaboration improves when both groups share goals around:

- Customer value
- Reliability
- Safe deployment
- Fast recovery
- Quality
- Learning

### 4. Security as an Enabler

John’s transformation is one of the book’s most important role-based lessons.

Security becomes more effective when it moves from:

| From | To |
|---|---|
| Late audit gate | Early design partner |
| Manual evidence only | Automated controls and evidence |
| One-off findings | Reusable patterns |
| Compliance-only mindset | Risk-informed delivery |
| Blocking language | Enabling language |

---

## Optional Activity: The Batch Size Game

Use coins, envelopes, index cards, or sticky notes to demonstrate small-batch flow.

### Instructions

1. Create a simple three-step process, such as write, review, approve.
2. Run it once with a large batch of 20 items.
3. Run it again with smaller batches of 3–5 items.
4. Compare speed, error detection, and waiting time.

### Debrief

Ask:

- Which approach found errors earlier?
- Which had less waiting?
- Which felt easier to control?
- How does this apply to software, project work, requirements, access, or change management?

## Key Plot Points to Surface

- IT moves from scapegoat to strategic partner.

- The Chaos Monkey exercise tests recovery and learning.
- Leadership begins to understand IT as essential to business outcomes.
- Project Phoenix helps Parts Unlimited regain momentum.

---

## Key Teaching Points

### 1. The Third Way Is Continual Learning

The Third Way is about creating a culture that supports experimentation, learning, practice, and improvement.

This includes:

- Blameless post-mortems
- Repetition and practice
- Controlled experiments
- Time for improvement work
- Knowledge sharing
- Resilience engineering
- Psychological safety

### 2. Failure Can Be Useful if It Produces Learning

Failure is not the goal. Learning is the goal.

Ask:

- Do people feel safe surfacing problems early?
- Are incidents used for blame or learning?
- Do we fix root causes or only symptoms?
- Do we practice recovery before major failure?

### 3. IT Is a Business Capability

The book ends by showing that IT is not merely a cost center. Technology delivery, reliability, security, and speed directly affect business performance.

### 4. Improvement Must Be Small Enough to Start

Participants should not propose massive transformations as their final action. They should propose one realistic change that can be tried in 30 days.

## Final Capstone: 30-Day Improvement Proposal

Participants should create a short proposal or pitch on one specific process change.

### Proposal Template

**Problem Statement**  
Describe one recurring problem that affects flow, quality, risk, reliability, or delivery.

**Current Impact**  
Explain how the problem affects time, cost, quality, morale, customer experience, or risk.

**Book Connection**  
Identify the related lesson from *The Phoenix Project*.

**Root Cause**  
Explain why the issue happens.

**Proposed 30-Day Improvement**  
Describe one small, realistic improvement that could be tested within 30 days.

**Expected Outcome**  
Explain what should improve.

**Measurement**  
Identify one or two simple success measures.

---

## Examples of Strong 30-Day Proposals

- Add access planning questions to project intake to reduce emergency access requests.
- Add operational readiness review before development starts for major releases.
- Create a Kanban lane for unplanned work and review it weekly.
- Add security requirements to design templates.
- Document and cross-train one process currently dependent on a single expert.
- Add rollback planning to change records.
- Review recurring support tickets monthly and feed them into product backlog.
- Automate evidence collection for one compliance control.
- Create office hours for expert support to reduce constant interruptions.

---

Each participant gets 3–5 minutes.

1. My role
2. Problem I identified
3. Book concept connected to the problem
4. Proposed 30-day improvement
5. Expected benefit
6. How success will be measured

# Common Facilitation Risks and Responses

## Risk: Discussion Becomes a Complaint Session

Response:

> “Let’s convert that into a problem statement. What condition causes this, who is impacted, and what could be improved?”

## Risk: One Person Dominates

Response:

> “I want to pause there and hear from someone in a different role.”

## Risk: Participants Stay Too Abstract

Response:

> “Can you give one real example from your work, without naming sensitive systems or people?”

## Risk: Participants Jump to Solutions Too Quickly

Response:

> “Before we solve it, let’s identify the constraint and the missing feedback loop.”

## Risk: Leaders in the Room Silence Others

Response:

Use silent writing or anonymous sticky notes before open discussion.

# Final Facilitator Closing Statement

> *The Phoenix Project* is not really a story about one failed IT project. It is a story about how organizations create chaos when work is invisible, priorities are unclear, feedback is slow, and learning is sacrificed for urgency. The value of this study is not whether we enjoyed the book. The value is whether we can now see our own work system more clearly and improve one part of it from where we sit.
